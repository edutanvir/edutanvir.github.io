Oil and gas. One connected view.

The short version

One page, the whole supply chain.

I built an Executive Overview report in Microsoft Fabric and Power BI that puts oil production, the average oil price, inventory, suppliers and shipping delays side by side. Pick a supplier, a risk level or a date range, and you can see how the pieces connect.

This write-up follows the oil in the order it moves. You don't need to be a data engineer to read it. I'll explain the jargon as we go.

Why this was worth building

Each number only tells part of the story.

Production tells you how much oil is coming out. Price tells you what it is worth. Inventory tells you what is sitting in storage, and shipping tells you what is on its way and whether it is late. Look at any one of them alone and you miss how they affect each other.

So the goal was simple: put all four on one page, so a reader can follow the oil from supplier to destination without switching between reports.

In plain English: what's a slicer?

A slicer is a filter you can see on the page, such as tick boxes or a date range. On this report the supplier, risk level and production date boxes are slicers. Use them to narrow the charts to the part of the picture you care about.

Three things worth a look

The headline numbers

Three cards at the top, Total Production, Avg Oil Price and Total Inventory, give the big picture at a glance.

Suppliers and the trend

The bar chart ranks the four suppliers in the data by production, and the line chart shows how production rises and falls across the dates you select.

Where it ships

The pie chart shows shipping days by destination country and delay status, so you can compare destinations and see where the delays sit.

What it's for

Questions you can ask it.

Who supplies the most?

Which suppliers account for the most production, and does that change when you narrow the dates or the risk level?

How does output move?

How does production rise and fall across the dates you select, and which stretches stand out?

Where are the delays?

How do shipping days split between Canada and the USA, and how much of that is delayed?

These are questions to explore, not proof of cause and effect. Always read a number alongside its source, units and date range.

Limitations and next steps

No report is ever finished.

A report is only as clear as the definitions behind it. If I kept going, I'd do three things:

  • Document the data. Write down where each table comes from, when it was loaded, and what each column means.
  • Label the numbers. Show units and currency on the headline cards, and say how each total is calculated.
  • Make it repeatable. Write down the steps that prepare the data and add checks that run the same way every time.
A report is only as trustworthy as the definitions underneath it.
