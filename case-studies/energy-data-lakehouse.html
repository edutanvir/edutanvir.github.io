The short version

One dashboard, many energy datasets.

I took a handful of public energy datasets, cleaned them up in a Microsoft Fabric lakehouse using PySpark, and built a Power BI report on top. The result is a dashboard where you can click through energy generation, CO₂ and prices by country, year and energy source.

This write-up follows the data on its journey, in the order it travels. You don't need to be a data engineer to read it. I'll explain the jargon as we go.

Why this was worth building

Energy data is trickier than it looks.

You'd think putting energy numbers side by side would be simple. It isn't. Different sources name the same country in different ways, measure in different units, and cover different years. Combine them without fixing that, and you get a chart that looks convincing but quietly misleads.

So most of the real work happens before anyone opens a dashboard: making sure dates, country names, energy-source labels and units all mean the same thing everywhere.

In plain English: what's a lakehouse?

Picture a lake and a warehouse side by side. The lake holds raw files exactly as they arrived. The warehouse holds tidy, checked tables that people actually query. A lakehouse keeps both in one place, so nobody has to copy data between systems just to analyse it.

These are questions to explore, not proof of cause and effect. Always read a number alongside its source, units, date range and coverage.

Limitations and next steps

No data project is ever finished.

Energy datasets differ in reporting periods, coverage, units and definitions, so any comparison needs a second look before you trust it. If I kept going, I'd do three things:

  • Document the sources. Write down each dataset's name, publisher, the date I downloaded it, and what its columns mean.
  • Double-check the measures. Confirm the units, how numbers are added up, which periods are missing, and what each headline number really represents.
  • Make it repeatable. Write down the transformation steps and add data-quality checks that run the same way every time.
A dashboard is only as trustworthy as the data work underneath it.
